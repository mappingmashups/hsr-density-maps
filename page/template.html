Where would High Speed Rail work in North America?

Metro populations and distances between many city pairs in North America are comparable to those in Europe, where High Speed Rail is successful. But North American cities are less dense, so fewer of their residents live close to where the train actually stops. This page measures that with one global population grid, using fixed circles around every station on 26 corridors.

Outlined bars: each station's share of its official metro area, the figure usually quoted for a city (US census metro areas, Statistics Canada metro areas, ABS Greater Sydney / Melbourne and urban areas). Stations sharing an area split it evenly. US maps show county outlines, with each station's metro area tinted.

How to read this

Each bar is a station's catchment. Its length is the number of people counted there, and its thickness is the stretch of line the catchment covers: up to the radius each way, but only halfway to the next station, so crowded stations get thin bars that touch. Both sides use one population scale.

Flip either map to run it from the other end. Slide the shorter route up or down (drag its map or bars, use the slider, or align the two biggest stations) to line up the cities you want to compare. Hover a bar or a station to see it on both.

On the maps, coloured circle area is proportional to the people counted (the same scale as the corridor maps below), and dashed outlines are the real catchments. The grey background is population density within 50 km of the line, shown for geography only.

How to read this

Coloured circle area is proportional to the people within the radius of each station. Dashed rings show the real radius at map scale, and the grey inside them shows how densely that land is populated. A coloured circle bigger than its dashed ring means the area inside the radius is denser than the circle scale; smaller means sparser.

Where two stations' rings overlap, a dashed line splits them halfway between the stations, matching how the counts work: each person is counted once, toward the nearest station. The arrow on each panel points north. Circle scale differs between radius views (each image has its own key) so the 5 km circles stay readable.

Station explorer

Every stop on one line: its 20 km footprint and its numbers.

“Counted” columns credit overlapping circles to the nearest station, and corridor totals use these. HRSL is Meta's 30 m layer, listed as a check on Kontur (it has no data for Canada, Australia or China). km to next is the straight-line distance.

Method

Each population cell counts for a station if its centre falls within the radius (great-circle distance). Where circles overlap, as at Tokyo/Shinagawa, Taipei/Banqiao, the LA basin and NYC/Newark, each cell goes to its nearest station only, so nobody is counted twice.

The maps use an azimuthal-equidistant projection centred on each corridor, rotated so the route runs vertically. 100 km is the same length in every panel.

Grey shading uses the Kontur hexagons inside each radius, in four density classes: 300–1,500, 1,500–5,000, 5,000–15,000 and 15,000+ people per km². Land under 300 per km² is left blank. The lowest cut-off matches the EU/GHSL definition of an urban cluster, and 1,500 matches an urban centre.

Data

  • Kontur Population 2023-11-01: a global H3 res-8 grid (~0.74 km² cells) fusing GHSL, Meta HRSL, Microsoft buildings and Copernicus land cover. It's the only uniform layer here that also covers Canada.
  • Meta / CIESIN HRSL: 30 m, used as a cross-check. It agrees with Kontur within a few percent wherever both exist.
  • Official metro populations: US Census Bureau metro areas and county estimates, Statistics Canada metro areas, and Australian Bureau of Statistics capital-city and urban areas.
  • 2050 projections: WorldPop's 1 km population projections under the middle-of-the-road scenario (SSP2). They're too coarse to count a 5 km circle directly, so each catchment's projected growth from 2025 to 2050 is applied to today's Kontur count.
  • Natural Earth 10 m outlines for the map backgrounds.

Caveats

  • Planned station sites are approximate: city centre or the most-cited candidate. The 5 km figures are sensitive to this. Texas Central's Houston terminal is at Northwest Mall, not downtown.
  • Windsor–Québec joins the Alto project (Toronto–Québec City) to the older Windsor–Toronto proposal.
  • Vancouver–Eugene uses the Amtrak Cascades stops as stand-ins for a future high-speed line.
  • Chicago–St. Louis, Chicago–Detroit and Chicago–Twin Cities use today's Amtrak stops (the latter via Madison, as in the Midwest regional rail plan). Atlanta–Charlotte and Cleveland–Columbus–Cincinnati have no fixed stations, so most sites are city centres.
  • Mumbai–Ahmedabad and the smaller Beijing–Shanghai stations are placed from approximate coordinates and may be a few km off; many of these stations sit outside town centres, so treat their 5 km figures with care.
  • Counts are residents only. Jobs, visitors and transfer catchments aren't included.
  • 2050 figures are modelled, not forecasts for particular neighbourhoods: WorldPop spreads each country's projected growth across the map using today's settlement pattern and trends. US metro-area figures for 2050 come from Hauer et al.'s county projections (SSP2): each county's latest estimate grown at its projected 2025–2050 rate (Connecticut, which replaced its counties in 2022, uses the state rate). Canadian and Australian metro figures for 2050 use today's official number scaled by WorldPop's growth within 50 km of the station.
